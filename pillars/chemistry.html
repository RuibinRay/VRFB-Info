One element, four oxidation states

Most batteries use two different materials for their two sides. The vanadium flow battery uses the same element on both, in two different pairs of oxidation states. The negative tank holds V²⁺ and V³⁺; the positive tank holds VO²⁺ (vanadium +4) and VO₂⁺ (vanadium +5), all dissolved in sulfuric acid.[1][2]

This is the single idea that makes the VRFB practical. Every other flow chemistry suffers when a little of one electrolyte leaks through the membrane into the other, poisoning it for good. In a VRFB the leaked ion is still vanadium; it just needs a periodic rebalancing to restore the two tanks to the same state of charge. The electrolyte itself never wears out.[2][16]

The half-reactions

During discharge, the negative electrode gives up electrons and the positive electrode takes them in. Written in the discharge direction:[1][9]

Negative electrode:  V²⁺ → V³⁺ + e⁻                           E° = −0.255 V vs SHE
Positive electrode:   VO₂⁺ + 2H⁺ + e⁻ → VO²⁺ + H₂O    E° = +1.004 V vs SHE
Overall:               V²⁺ + VO₂⁺ + 2H⁺ → V³⁺ + VO²⁺ + H₂O   E°cell = 1.259 V Standard reduction potentials for the vanadium couples; charging runs every arrow backwards.

Notice the protons. Two H⁺ are consumed on the positive side every time an electron is delivered, so protons must cross the membrane from the negative side to keep both electrolytes electrically neutral. That is the membrane's whole job: let H⁺ (and some water) through, keep vanadium out.[4]

Advanced · why the electrons move

The driving force is the difference in the two couples' electrode potentials. Each is set by the Gibbs free energy of its reduction, ΔG = −nFE. With n = 1 for both vanadium couples, the cell delivers 1.259 V × 96 485 C/mol ≈ 121 kJ per mole of electrons, or about 34 Wh per mole of vanadium transferred. Kinetically the V²⁺/V³⁺ couple is a simple outer-sphere electron transfer; the VO²⁺/VO₂⁺ couple requires an oxygen transfer and has the slower kinetics, which is why most electrode-activation research targets the positive side.[10]

Voltage depends on state of charge: the Nernst equation

A fully charged VRFB sits at a higher voltage than a nearly empty one. The relationship is the Nernst equation, which adds a concentration-dependent term to the standard potential. For the overall cell, writing the state of charge as SOC (the fraction of vanadium in the charged form on each side):[9][15]

E = E° + (RT/F) · ln [ (cV²⁺ · cVO₂⁺ · cH⁺²) / (cV³⁺ · cVO²⁺) ]
  = E° + (RT/F) · [ 2 ln( SOC / (1 − SOC) ) + 2 ln cH⁺ ] R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, F = 96 485 C mol⁻¹, T in kelvin. RT/F = 25.7 mV at 25 °C.

Measured open-circuit voltages are consistently about 50–60 mV higher than this equation predicts at 50 % SOC (≈ 1.40 V rather than ≈ 1.34 V). Knehr and Kumbur showed the gap comes from proton activity and the Donnan potential at the membrane, which the simple form ignores.[15] The explorer below lets you add that empirical offset.

The curve is steep near 0 % and 100 % SOC: this is why battery-management systems keep the operating window at roughly 10–90 %, and why OCV is a useful SOC sensor in the middle of the range but a poor one at the extremes.

The electrolyte

Commercial electrolyte is roughly 1.5–2 mol/L of vanadium in 2–5 mol/L sulfuric acid. The vanadium concentration sets how much energy a litre can hold; the acid supplies the protons, keeps the vanadium dissolved and makes the solution conductive.[2]

Energy density is modest: around 25 Wh per litre of total electrolyte at 1.6 M, roughly a tenth of a lithium-ion cell. That is acceptable for stationary storage, where the tanks simply get bigger, but it rules out vehicles.[2][5]

Advanced · solubility and temperature

Vanadium concentration is capped by solubility. V²⁺ and V³⁺ sulfates precipitate at low temperature (below ≈ 10 °C for ~2 M solutions), while VO₂⁺ forms solid V₂O₅ above ≈ 40 °C when the battery is highly charged. Practical systems therefore run between 10 and 40 °C and avoid parking the positive electrolyte at very high SOC in hot weather.[2][11] The mixed-acid electrolyte developed at PNNL (sulfate + chloride) raises the usable concentration to about 2.5 M and widens the window to −5 to 50 °C, a 70 % energy-density gain.[3] Roe et al. pushed sulfate electrolyte to 3 M with phosphate stabilisers.[18]

What can go wrong chemically

  • Hydrogen evolution. At the negative electrode near full charge, some current splits water into H₂ instead of reducing V³⁺. It wastes charge and slowly unbalances the tanks.[2][16]
  • Air oxidation. V²⁺ reacts with oxygen, so the negative tank is blanketed with nitrogen or kept sealed.[2]
  • Crossover. Vanadium ions diffuse through the membrane, self-discharging the cell and moving volume from one tank to the other. Unlike other chemistries this is reversible by remixing or electrochemical rebalancing.[4][16]
  • Carbon corrosion. VO₂⁺ at high SOC can oxidise the positive felt and the bipolar plate, so stacks are rarely charged past about 95 %.[10]
