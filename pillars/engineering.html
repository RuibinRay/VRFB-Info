Decoupling power from energy

In a conventional battery, power and energy scale together: more cells means more of both. In a flow battery they are separate purchases. Power (kW) is set by the total electrode area in the stacks, since current scales with area at a chosen current density. Energy (kWh) is set by how much vanadium is dissolved in the tanks.[2][4]

P ≈ Ncells · A · j · Vcell      E ≈ 2 · Vtank · cV · F · ΔSOC · Vnominal / 2 A: active area per cell · j: current density · cV: vanadium concentration · ΔSOC: usable window · the factor ½ appears because each electron needs one vanadium ion on each side.

The practical consequence: a 4-hour system and a 10-hour system can share identical stacks and differ only in tank size. Longer durations get cheaper per kWh, which is the VRFB's commercial niche.[5]

Model notes and formulas

Stack current I = j·A. Electrolyte per side = E / (c · 26.8 Ah/mol · ΔSOC · Vcell). Stoichiometric flow per stack = N·I / (F·c); the pump delivers λ times that. Pump power = Q·Δp / η for two loops per stack. Voltage efficiency uses a lumped area-specific resistance around an open-circuit voltage of 1.40 V: VE = (OCV − j·ASR)/(OCV + j·ASR). System efficiency multiplies cell efficiency by (1 − pump fraction) and the inverter efficiency twice. Shunt currents, thermal management and the SOC-dependence of ASR are neglected; published system values of 65–80 % AC-AC are consistent with this model at 60–100 mA/cm².[5][14]

Where the energy goes

Three efficiencies multiply to give the battery's round-trip figure.[4][14]

  • Coulombic efficiency (95–98 %): charge lost to crossover, shunt currents and hydrogen evolution.
  • Voltage efficiency (80–92 %): the voltage gap between charging and discharging caused by resistance and reaction kinetics. It falls as current density rises, so running harder trades efficiency for a smaller, cheaper stack.
  • System losses: pumps (2–5 %) and the inverter (2–4 % each way).

Put together, cells reach 80–90 % DC and complete systems 65–80 % AC-to-AC, lower than lithium-ion's 85–90 % but stable over tens of thousands of cycles.[5]

Advanced · polarisation

Cell voltage under load is V = OCV ± (ηact + ηohm + ηconc). Activation overpotential follows Butler–Volmer kinetics and is dominated by the positive electrode; ohmic loss is the membrane plus electrolyte plus contact resistance (together ≈ 1–2 Ω·cm² in commercial cells); concentration overpotential appears when the flow factor is too low to replenish vanadium at the fibre surface, especially near the ends of the SOC window. Flow-field plates and thinner, compressed felts reduce all three.[10][14]

Thermal and flow management

Resistive heating and pump work warm the electrolyte. Because VO₂⁺ precipitates above about 40 °C and V²⁺/V³⁺ sulfates below about 10 °C, large systems add a heat exchanger and keep the pumps running during idle periods in hot weather to stop the stack from cooking.[2][11]

Variable-speed pumps scheduled against state of charge are a cheap efficiency win: high flow is needed only near the ends of the SOC window, where vanadium concentration at the electrode runs low.[14]

Scaling up

Commercial stacks run from a few kilowatts to roughly 30–40 kW each, with cell areas of 0.1–0.4 m² and 20–100 cells per stack. Megawatt systems connect many stacks in series–parallel to reach a DC bus voltage of several hundred volts, then share tanks that hold hundreds of cubic metres of electrolyte.[14][16] The Dalian station reaches 100 MW and 400 MWh this way, using containerised stacks fed from a tank farm.[7]
