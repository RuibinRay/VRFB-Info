From a single cell to a power station

A VRFB is three things bolted together: a stack that converts chemistry to current, tanks that store the chemistry, and a balance of plant (pumps, pipes, power electronics and controls) that moves liquid and electrons between them. The stack decides power in kilowatts; the tanks decide energy in kilowatt-hours; the two can be sized independently.[2][4]

Inside the stack, every cell is a sandwich: a membrane between two carbon-felt electrodes, each backed by a bipolar plate. Cells are stacked electrically in series and hydraulically in parallel, fed from manifolds that run the length of the stack.[4]

A copper sheet with a terminal tab that gathers current from the full electrode area. It sits dry, behind the first bipolar plate, because copper corrodes rapidly in the acid electrolyte.

Flow frame & gasket

A polymer frame holds the felt in its window and routes electrolyte from the manifold holes in the corners into the electrode. Elastomer gaskets on each face seal the cell. Long, narrow channels deliberately add resistance to limit shunt currents through the shared manifold.

Frame
PVC · PP · PVDF
Gasket
EPDM · Viton® · PTFE
Thickness
Sets felt compression, 20–30 %

Bipolar plate

A graphite–polymer composite plate shared by neighbouring cells: positive face of one, negative face of the next. Electrons pass through it so cells connect in series. The serpentine flow field spreads electrolyte across the felt and cuts pumping losses by 30–50 % versus flow-through designs.

Material
Graphite-filled PP / PE / PVDF, 2–6 mm
Resistivity
10⁻³–10⁻² Ω·cm
Flow field
Serpentine · interdigitated · flow-through

Sources [4][10][14]

Negative electrode (carbon felt)

A porous mat of 8–12 µm carbon fibres, 90–95 % porosity, where V²⁺/V³⁺ exchange electrons with the fibre surface. Felts are activated by heating in air or acid treatment, which adds oxygen groups that accelerate the reaction.

Material
PAN- or rayon-based carbon felt
Thickness
3–6 mm, compressed 20–30 %
Fibre diameter
≈ 10 µm
Side reaction
H₂ evolution near full charge

Sources [2][10]

Ion-exchange membrane

A 25–200 µm polymer film that conducts protons and blocks vanadium. Perfluorosulfonic-acid membranes (Nafion®) are the benchmark; cheaper hydrocarbon and anion-exchange membranes trade some selectivity or conductivity for cost. It is usually the most expensive single component of the stack.

Material
PFSA (Nafion® 115/212) · hydrocarbon · AEM
Area resistance
0.5–2 Ω·cm²
Crossover
Causes self-discharge; corrected by rebalancing

Sources [2][4][14]

Positive electrode (carbon felt)

The mirror of the negative felt, hosting VO²⁺/VO₂⁺. Kinetics are slower here, so catalysts (bismuth, metal oxides) and surface functionalisation are active research areas. At high SOC the strongly oxidising VO₂⁺ can corrode the carbon itself.

Material
Carbon or graphite felt, O-functionalised
Limit
Keep below ≈ 95 % SOC

Sources [2][10]

Tie rods and clamp brackets

Threaded rods through the corner holes pull the end plates together; external brackets keep the plates flat. Tightened in a star pattern, like a wheel, to seal evenly.

Material
Stainless steel with insulating sleeves
Count
4–12 per stack

Advanced · shunt currents

Because every cell shares the same conductive electrolyte through the manifolds, a parallel electrical path exists around each cell. The resulting shunt currents waste charge and corrode plates near the ends of the stack. Designers lengthen the inlet channels in each flow frame, limit the number of cells per manifold and sometimes split long stacks into hydraulically separate modules to keep this loss to a few percent.[4][14]

How stacks fail, and how they are maintained

  • Leaks from gasket creep or uneven clamping: the most common field fault; fixed by re-torquing or gasket replacement.[14]
  • Membrane fouling or degradation after years of service raises resistance; membranes are replaced during a stack rebuild, typically after 10–15 years.[5][14]
  • Capacity fade from crossover and side reactions is corrected by rebalancing the electrolytes, not by replacing them.[16]
  • Pump and seal wear on the balance of plant is ordinary industrial maintenance.

Because the electrolyte is recoverable at end of life, a VRFB installation retains residual value that lithium-ion systems do not. That matters for the economics, covered in the next pillar.
